Listening to the brain for 100 years

Your brain runs on electricity. Billions of nerve cells signal to each other all the time, and when large groups of them work in step, a tiny electrical ripple reaches the skin of your head. EEG (electro-encephalo-graphy: "electric brain writing") picks up that ripple with small sensors and draws it as a wave. No needles, no radiation, and it doesn't hurt.

How it works

From nerve cells to a wave on the screen

Step 1

Cells fire together

Millions of nerve cells near the brain's surface, lined up side by side, send signals at the same moment.

Step 2

It spreads out

The signal travels through brain, fluid, skull and skin. Bone smears it sideways, so it arrives blurred and very weak.

Step 3

Sensors listen

Small sensors rest on the scalp: 32 or 128 of them in the nets we use. Each one measures the voltage under it.

Step 4

Amplify and draw

An amplifier makes the signal about 10,000 times bigger, and a computer draws one wave per sensor, hundreds of times a second.

The sensors

Three ways to touch the scalp

Hair and dry skin block electricity, so every scalp sensor (an electrode) needs a good contact with the skin. There are three main kinds. Gel has long been the standard; newer kinds trade a little signal quality for speed and comfort.21,22

Many forms

One idea, many kinds of EEG

All EEG measures the same thing: the brain's electrical activity. What changes is how many sensors are used, where they sit, and how long the recording runs.

Simplified drawings, not to scale.

How small is it?

A brain wave is about 30,000 times weaker than a torch battery

Swipe the scale sideways on a phone. Each grid step is ten times smaller than the one to its left. Brain responses to a single sound are so small that we have to average many repeats to see them (see below).

Brain rhythms

The brain hums at different speeds

Scientists sort brain waves by how many times they go up and down each second, measured in hertz (Hz). Different speeds tend to show up in different states.8 Every brain shows all of them in some mix, all day.

Why use EEG?

EEG is the fastest way to watch a working brain

When vs where. MRI scanners show where in the brain something happens very sharply, but they are slow: they measure blood flow, which takes seconds to respond. EEG tells when something happens, to the thousandth of a second, but the skull blurs where.

Simple and safe. EEG is silent, uses no radiation or magnets, works with children and older adults, and costs far less than a scanner. That makes it practical for following the same families over many years.

Positions in the chart are approximate, to show the trade-off. Real values depend on the equipment and the question.

The averaging trick

Finding a whisper in a crowd

When you hear a sound, your brain replies within a few hundred milliseconds. That reply is smaller than the brain's ongoing hum, so in a single try you cannot see it. Play the sound many times and average the recordings: the random hum cancels out and the reply stays. These replies are called event-related potentials (ERPs). Averaging has been used since the 1950s.6 Try it with the see-through sheets in the demos below.

What it's used for

From the hospital ward to the research lab

Clinic

Epilepsy

EEG shows the electrical storms of seizures. In 1935 doctors saw a "3 per second spike-and-wave" pattern in children with absence seizures.3 It is still the main test for epilepsy.

Clinic

Sleep

Each stage of sleep has its own waves. EEG revealed dreaming (REM) sleep in 19535 and is part of every overnight sleep study.

Clinic

Intensive care and surgery

EEG monitors brain activity in unconscious patients and helps check depth of anaesthesia.

Research

Attention and surprise

A large wave about 300 ms after an unexpected sound or picture (the "P300") was found in 19657. It tracks how the brain notices and updates.

Research

Filtering out repeats

Hear two clicks half a second apart, and a healthy brain responds less to the second one. In 1982 researchers found this filtering is weaker in many people with schizophrenia.10

Research

Choosing treatments

In 2020 a study of 309 people with depression found an EEG pattern that predicted who improved on one antidepressant.16 Research like this aims to match people to treatments faster.

Research

Families and risk

Some EEG patterns run in families. Studying patients, their relatives and healthy volunteers helps separate signs of an illness from signs of inherited risk.

Research

Brain–computer interfaces

Since 19739, scientists have used EEG to let people control a computer cursor or spell words with their brain signals, which helps people who cannot move.

What EEG cannot do

  • It cannot read your thoughts. It measures the overall electrical activity of large groups of cells, not ideas or memories.
  • It is not a test for mental illness on its own. Psychiatric diagnosis still comes from a clinician talking with you. EEG research looks for patterns across many people; one person's EEG cannot say whether they have a condition.
  • It does not change your brain. EEG only listens; nothing is sent into your head.
Timeline

151 years of brain waves, from rabbits to AI

Animal first shown in animalsHuman human studies and clinicTools methods, machines and data

Discovery in animals · 1875–1914

1875
Animal

Richard Caton records the first brain currents

Working in Liverpool, he measured electrical currents from the exposed brains of rabbits and monkeys, and saw them change when light shone in the eyes.1

1890
Animal

Adolf Beck sees activity pause with a stimulus

In rabbits and dogs, the ongoing activity stopped when the animal was stimulated, an early sign that waves reflect what the brain is doing.1

1912
Animal

The first photograph of brain waves

Vladimir Pravdich-Neminsky photographed a dog's brain waves with a string galvanometer, the instrument then used for heart recordings.1

1914
Animal

A seizure captured on paper

Napoleon Cybulski photographed the brain activity of a seizure in a dog, pointing towards EEG's future role in epilepsy.1

The first human recordings · 1924–1937

1924
Human

Hans Berger records a human brain

On 6 July 1924, in Jena, Germany, the psychiatrist Hans Berger made the first recording of a person's brain waves.2

1929
Human

Alpha and beta waves named

Berger published his results after five years of checking, and named the alpha rhythm (seen with eyes closed) and the faster beta rhythm.2

1934
Human

Scientists confirm Berger

Many doubted Berger until Edgar Adrian and Bryan Matthews repeated his findings in Cambridge, showing the alpha rhythm stops when the eyes open.1,2

1935
Human

EEG enters the clinic for epilepsy

Gibbs, Davis and Lennox showed a distinct pattern in children with absence seizures. Clinical EEG labs opened soon after.3

1937
Human

Sleep has stages

Alfred Loomis and colleagues recorded EEG through whole nights and found that sleep passes through distinct stages.3

Standards, sleep and the mind · 1947–1982

1947
Tools

The field organises

The first International EEG Congress met in London. Its successors set common rules, including the "10-20" map of sensor positions still used worldwide, published in 1958.2,4

1951
Tools

Averaging reveals hidden responses

George Dawson added up many repeats to pull small brain responses out of the background, the idea behind every ERP study since.2,6

1953
Human

Dreaming sleep discovered

Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman found regular periods of rapid eye movement during sleep, with a waking-like EEG: REM sleep.5

1965
Human

The P300: the brain's "surprise" wave

Samuel Sutton and colleagues found a large positive wave about 300 ms after an uncertain or unexpected stimulus.7

1973
Tools

"Brain–computer interface" is coined

Jacques Vidal proposed using EEG for direct communication between brain and computer.9

1978
Human

Mismatch negativity

Risto Näätänen and colleagues showed the brain automatically flags an odd sound in a sequence, even when you are not paying attention. It became a widely studied marker in schizophrenia research.17

1982
Human

Sensory filtering in schizophrenia

Using two clicks half a second apart, Lawrence Adler, Robert Freedman and colleagues reported weaker filtering of the second click in schizophrenia: the "P50" test used in our own research.10

Mapping and computing · 1993–2004

1993
Tools

Dense sensor nets

Don Tucker described the geodesic sensor net, which spreads 64 to 256 sponge sensors evenly over the head. Our nets are this design.11

1994
Tools

Estimating where signals come from

Methods such as LORETA began to estimate the brain sources behind scalp patterns, within the limits the skull allows.12

1997
Tools

Magnetic stimulation meets EEG

Researchers first recorded the brain's EEG response to a magnetic pulse (TMS-EEG), a way to test how brain circuits react.2

2004
Tools

Free software for everyone

EEGLAB, a free, open toolbox, made advanced EEG analysis available to labs worldwide.13

Open science, prediction and AI · 2013–2026

2013
Human

An EEG aid for ADHD assessment

The US FDA allowed an EEG-based test as an aid, alongside a full clinical assessment, for ADHD in children aged 6 to 17.18 Its usefulness is still debated.

2019
Tools

A common language for sharing data

EEG-BIDS gave labs a standard way to organise and share recordings, so studies can be combined and checked.14

2020
Human

EEG predicts antidepressant response

A machine-learning study of 309 people found a resting EEG pattern that predicted improvement on sertraline, and it held up at other sites.16 The same year, a method to separate rhythms from the brain's background "1/f" activity changed how researchers measure brain waves.15

2024
Human

EEG turns 100

Clinicians and scientists marked a century since Berger's first human recording.2

2025–26
Tools

AI "foundation models" for EEG

Large AI models are being trained on very large collections of EEG, much as language models learn from text, then adapted to tasks such as spotting seizures. Early results were presented at international meetings in 2026.19 They are promising but not yet proven in everyday care, and wearable, quicker-to-fit EEG is growing alongside them.20
