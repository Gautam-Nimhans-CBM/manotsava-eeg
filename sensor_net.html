What else the sensors pick up13

The two diamonds are the bony landmarks every cap is lined up against: the nasion, the dip between your eyes, and the inion, the bump at the back of your skull. You can feel both on yourself. Electrode positions are measured out from them, which is how one net lands in the same place on different heads.4 The dots are real electrode positions from the Geodesic Sensor Net1,2 — note how far it reaches below the ear line and onto the cheeks, where it picks up eye movement. The dashed ring marks where the signal has dropped to half its strongest value: press Take the skull away and watch it shrink. Bone conducts electricity roughly fifteen times worse than the brain and scalp around it, and that is what smears the signal sideways.7,8

How this model works, and where it comes from

Each tap places one small patch of synchronised activity (a current dipole, pointing straight out of the head) inside a single uniform conductor.5 Its field reaches every sensor at once — at these frequencies there is no travel time.6 The skull is not drawn as a separate layer; its smearing is imitated by making the source act as if it sat a little deeper than it does, and the slider sets how much. Real heads have layered skull, scalp and fluid, and the true amount of blur differs from person to person, so the numbers here are illustrative. The head is the standard net template scaled about 5% smaller, to match the mean head length and breadth measured in 104 adults from Meghalaya, Northeast India;3 the inion is placed on the back midline opposite the nasion, not taken from a scan.

Brain rhythms. The five bands follow the clinical definitions.17 Each button moves the source to where that rhythm is classically strongest and sets its size to a typical scalp amplitude. Real rhythms come from many sources at once, change with age and state, and differ between people; one source per band is a simplification.

The rival spot. Working backwards from scalp readings to a source has no unique answer: different arrangements inside the head can give almost the same pattern outside.9,10 The button searches about 5,000 other positions for the one whose pattern matches best, lets that rival choose its own strength, and then asks whether what is left over would stand out from background EEG of 10 µV at each sensor in a single sweep. That assumes the noise at each sensor is independent; real background EEG is shared between neighbouring sensors, so true separation is harder than shown. Averaging many sweeps, as in an ERP, is what shrinks that noise.

Why more sensors help. Extra sensors cannot undo the skull's blur, but they take more separate looks at the same pattern, so a small difference stands out sooner.1,8 We checked this in the model at 45 combinations of position, depth and blur: the 128-sensor net left a bigger difference to work with every time, typically about twice as big — close to the square root of having four times as many sensors.
