How this model works, and where it comes from

This is a slice through the head at the level of the sensor ring, seen from above with the nose at the top. The sensors are the twenty positions of the international 10-10 system that run around the head through the forehead, temples and back, each 18° apart.1 The gold dot is a small patch of synchronised activity (a current dipole) in a single uniform conductor, and its field reaches every sensor at once.2 The skull is not modelled as a separate layer: its sideways smearing, which comes from bone conducting electricity about fifteen times worse than brain and scalp,4 is imitated by making the source act as if it sat a little deeper. Depths assume a scalp radius of about 89 mm. All of it is simplified for demonstration.

Brain rhythms. The five bands follow the clinical definitions.6 Each button moves the source to where that rhythm is classically strongest and sets its size to a typical scalp amplitude. This slice runs below the top of the head, so theta (just in front of the crown) and beta (over the movement strip) are drawn at the nearest point in the slice. This page shows one moment, not the rhythm in time — the 3D sensor net shows the waves. Real rhythms come from many sources at once and vary between people; one source per band is a simplification.

Every answer that fits. Going backwards from scalp readings to a source has no unique answer.3,5 The shaded region shows every outward-facing source that, with its strength adjusted, would differ from the real readings by less than the background EEG left after averaging the chosen number of sweeps, as in an ERP: roughly 10 µV per sensor in one sweep, falling with the square root of the number averaged (3.2 µV for 10, 1 µV for 100). That assumes the noise at each sensor is independent; real background EEG is shared between neighbouring sensors, so in practice the region is larger.
